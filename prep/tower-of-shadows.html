Lead with this — it is the story of the book

Fantagraphics is publishing Marvel comics. Fantagraphics is the home of Love and Rockets, Hate, and above all The Comics Journal — the magazine that spent decades as the sharpest critic of exactly this kind of corporate comics. Gary Groth, its co-founder and the Journal's editor, is the supervising editor on a Marvel licensed reprint. Publishers Weekly covered the deal under the headline "Strange Bedfellows".

That is your episode's spine: the alt-comix publisher curating Marvel's unreprinted back catalogue, and doing it with essays and creator bios rather than a bare Epic Collection dump.

01Why this title exists at all

Straight from Michael Dean's introduction, and it is the best context in the book. The 1954 Comics Code — formed by the industry itself, in a panic over Wertham's Seduction of the Innocent and the Senate juvenile delinquency hearings — banned the word "horror", banned "gruesome illustrations", banned the walking dead, vampires, ghouls and "werewolfism", and required good to triumph. EC's Vault of Horror was outlawed by its own title. Mad survived only by becoming a magazine, which put it outside the Code's reach.

For fifteen years the only monsters allowed were matinee creatures and rubbery aliens — the Atlas monster comics, Tales to Astonish and Journey into Mystery. Then in 1968 DC started testing the water again with House of Mystery and House of Secrets.

The point worth making on air: the Code did not relent until 1971, when it finally allowed vampires and werewolves "when handled in the classic tradition". Marvel did not wait. Tower of Shadows launched in 1969 — ahead of permission — with Chamber of Darkness a month behind it. Everything that follows, Tomb of Dracula, Werewolf by Night, Ghost Rider, comes through the door these two books pushed open.

Dean's framing of the pair is good and worth stealing: Chamber is the sudden "Boo!", Tower is the dread of what is waiting in the dark.

02The Steranko fight

The lead story of issue #1 is the single most talked-about thing in this book, and it is a production story rather than a plot.

Jim Steranko wrote and drew "At the Stroke of Midnight!" — except he called it "The Lurking Fear at Shadow House", a deliberate Lovecraft homage. Stan Lee rejected his cover, and the two clashed over panel design, dialogue and the title. By Steranko's account Lee did not care for the Lovecraft reference and substituted his own title; one version of the story has Lee pushing for "Let Them Eat Cake". The rift was serious enough that Steranko stopped freelancing for Marvel — Lee phoned him about a month later once both had cooled off, and Steranko came back for covers in 1972–73.

  • It won a 1969 Alley Award for Best Feature Story — so the story Lee fought him over is the one the readers voted best of the year.
  • Great debate: whose book is it? Steranko was at the height of his reputation and Lee was still editor. Both were arguably right.
  • Look at the finished pages with this in mind — the argument was about panel design, so the storytelling itself is the evidence.

04The thread to pull: EC comes back

The Code killed EC. Fifteen years later Marvel opens a horror book and hires Wallace Wood, Johnny Craig and Marie Severin — all EC alumni. Wood alone has four stories here, more than anyone.

  • Wood is the most-represented creator in the book. If you want one through-line for the episode, he is it.
  • Marie Severin pencils or draws five of the nine covers — she is the visual identity of this title and is rarely given that credit.
  • Dan Adkins inks across five stories. Under-discussed connective tissue.

05Where the line is going

Useful for the wrap-up. Fantagraphics has said future Lost Marvels volumes will cover the 1980s Savage Tales, Marvel science fiction like Unknown Worlds of Science Fiction, and Doug Murray and Michael Golden's The 'Nam. Dean has said he hopes to collect Chamber of Darkness, this book's companion title.

06Questions to put to each other

  • Does Fantagraphics doing this change how the material reads, or is the packaging just nicer?
  • Is an anthology of one-off horror shorts actually reviewable the way a run is? What do you even rate?
  • The Code forced these stories into twist-ending shapes. Does the restriction make them better or just samey?
  • Steranko versus Lee — whose call should it have been?
  • This is a curated selection, not a complete run. Does leaving stories out make it a better book or an incomplete one?
Two things to check as you read. First, the stories are 1969–71 and some titles already signal dated content — "One Little Indian!" especially. Decide before recording how you want to handle it. Second, this is a selection: work out which stories from #1–9 are missing and whether the omissions look like quality calls or rights problems. That would be a genuinely original observation.
